How It Works

A bookkeeper, not a generator

Every other server in this ecosystem makes something — a background, a character, a song. This one makes almost nothing. It's the piece that keeps a branching story from falling apart, and that turns out to be a different job entirely.

A visual novel is a graph. Labels, jumps, menus, flags, and a sprite for every character in every mood. Miss one connection and a playthrough dead-ends somewhere no test will find, because the only way to reach it was a specific combination of earlier choices. So the server derives the graph by parsing the scripts rather than trusting anything written down about them: it reports dangling jumps, undefined flags and unresolved sprites the moment a scene is saved, while the context is still warm. Sixteen tools, and most of them exist to answer "is this still coherent?"

The manuscript is the master, and the game is downstream of it

I write the story in Word, because that's where writing happens. The game is Ren'Py. Two copies of the same prose is the oldest failure in adaptation work: the author edits the document, nobody notices the script didn't move, and the game quietly ships last week's wording.

So the document is compared against the script mechanically — block by block, 639 of them at last count — and a difference gets reported rather than assumed away. Where copy has to live inside the interface instead of in dialogue, as it does for the choice cards, it isn't typed into the interface at all; it's generated out of the document. When the player is asked "Which technique will you choose?" and weighs Wudang Sword Style against Divine Frost Yin Energy and the Dark Crescent Shadow Sword, those three descriptions came out of the manuscript on the last build. If the two ever disagree the tool refuses to write a partial screen, because a card silently missing its description is precisely the bug the arrangement exists to prevent.

A choice you weigh, not a line you pick

Ren'Py's built-in menu renders a choice as short strings over the background, which reads as dialogue — fine for answering someone, wrong for the first real decision in the story. The technique choice is three cards instead: the manual itself, its name, and what it costs you. All three are learnable eventually and they combine, so it's a question of order rather than a locked path, and the fight system reads whatever he's actually learned rather than a single stored answer.

The art comes from the rest of the ecosystem

Backgrounds from the Background Generator, characters from the Character & Panel Generator, the theme from the Music Generator. What this server adds is the bridge: matte a character once, then place hand-drawn face patches over that one body at pixel offsets. Bodies are never re-rendered per expression, which is the whole reason a character doesn't jitter when his mood changes mid-conversation.

Some things are drawn deterministically rather than generated, for the same reason as everywhere else in this toolkit — a model can't be asked reliably for exact geometry. Night backgrounds are one. Rather than dropping a blue tint over a daytime plate, the horizon is traced and the sky replaced, so night is genuinely night and the buildings underneath keep their own lit windows.

What Claude Code learned

Four things that cost real time

  • A tint is not a night. The obvious way to get a night version of a background is to darken the day one. It reads as a photograph with the brightness pulled down, because that's what it is — sky and lit windows dim together, and nothing in the picture believes it. Replacing the sky properly means finding where it ends, and no per-pixel threshold can do that: on the Demonic Cult city plate, sky and mountain measured identical in both brightness and texture, so every threshold either ate the mountain or left the clouds. What worked was tracing the ridge as a continuous contour and cutting along it. Four rounds of tuning failed first. The fix was a different question, not a better number.
  • Text inside an interface is invisible to a diff. The tool comparing document to script only sees dialogue. The three technique descriptions were written into the choice screen, which sits outside that — so they became a second master immediately, edited in the document and unchanged in the game, with nothing reporting it. Generating the screen from the document closed it. The general form: anything a consistency check can't see will drift, and you find out by eye, months later.
  • One sample can't tell a setting from a seed. This came up repeatedly and cost real time each go. A render came back badly blurred on wording that was already correct, and the fix was a re-roll, not a rewrite. A theory about why some character sheets produce a usable back view survived three examples and died on the fourth. Change one thing, hold the seed, and compare — or you'll confidently learn something untrue.
  • Local video can't animate cloth on flat cel art. Tested directly, hoping to give the promo video some life. What comes back isn't fabric moving — it's a smooth periodic displacement across the whole region, which is a heat-haze shader by another name. Cloth reads as cloth because folds have edges that travel as a unit, and nothing that slides pixels continuously imitates that. Flat color gives the model no fold structure to move, so it moves the pixels instead. The still image and an additive glow won.
Ecosystem

The assembler at the end of the chain

This is the newest piece of a wider webcomic and animation toolkit: the Background Generator for backgrounds, the Character & Panel Generator for consistent characters, Novel Translation for translating my novel into other languages, Music Generation for theme songs and BGM, and Anime Production for animated videos. A speech bubble server is still to come.

Where Anime Production turns finished art into a video, this one turns it into something you play. Same assets, same machine, different end product — and both of them assemble what the other servers made rather than making anything of their own.

For everyone's use

Get the server

It's open source (MIT), like the rest of the toolkit. Ask your AI harness to clone the webcomic-toolkit repository and follow the setup docs in visual-novel-mcp.

This is the cheapest server in the ecosystem to run — no model, no weights, no GPU. It parses scripts and keeps a manifest, so it works on any machine. Your AI harness installs Ren'Py itself as part of setup, including the extra component the browser preview needs, so there's nothing for you to download and no account to make. Bring the other servers when you want art and music; this one assembles what they produce.
