Nuclear Singapore

What if Singapore goes nuclear?

When history goes critical

As we consider nuclear power, here are some of the lessons we should learn from the past.

The Great Acceleration

The Anthropocene

The advent of nuclear technology coincides with the phenomenon known as the Great Acceleration, which is the dramatic and exponential surge in human activity that has visible and tremendous impact on the global environment. The Great Acceleration also marks the beginning of the Anthropocene epoch, though several scholars point to the burning of coal in the Industrial Revolution of the 18th century as the actual origins, particularly the advent of James Watt’s steam engine in 1784.[^30]

Regardless, the Anthropocene—defined as the geological epoch during which human activity becomes the dominant influence on the global climate and environment—remains an important term used in environmental humanities, even if it has yet to be officially recognized (we’re still in the Holocene). Even so, they acknowledge that the Anthropocene remains an invaluable descriptor in understanding the relationship between humans and the environment.[^31] What interests me is that while discussion around the Anthropocene is primarily centered on the increase in carbon content and carbon dioxide emissions, along with other greenhouse gases that lead to climate change and global warming, the spike in human activity that impacted the environment during the Great Acceleration also includes nuclear weapons testing, especially the hydrogen bomb experiments in the 1950s. The radioactive fallout is just as significant a marker of environmental impact by human activities as the burning of fossil fuels and carbon emissions. The Anthropocene Working Group also proposed a boundary marker at Crawford Lake, pointing toward the release of plutonium from hydrogen bomb tests during the 1950s.[^32]

The Anthropocene has spawned more perspectives in environmental humanities on how we should examine the relationship between humanity and the environment, such as Donna Haraway’s Chthulucene, which focuses on interspecies relationships and vast biodiversity that eliminates an ecological hierarchy where humans are ranked at the top. Similarly, Timothy Morton’s concept of the hyperobject is a way to explain how humans attempt to conceive an entity so vast and beyond human comprehension (such as Cthulhu, who can be used as an example of how we interpret hyperobjects as a cosmic horror of astronomical scale). The world doesn’t belong to us alone.

And radiation—nuclear power—can also be understood as a hyperobject. Radioactive materials such as uranium and plutonium have extremely long half-lives, extending to thousands or even billions of years, far beyond the lifespan of any human. To try and grasp this hyperobject of radiation, we often conceive it as monstrous—that is where films such as Godzilla or other kaiju movies, nuclear dystopian games, and science fiction novels and animations come in useful, as they tell us about humanity’s attempts to imagine the hyperobject known as radiation in a physical form we can find understandable.

Hibakusha

As we consider the pursuit of nuclear technology to combat climate change, we should also consider why the public may be resistant to it. Such resistance and reticence are best expressed in Hibakusha narratives—stories of the victims of nuclear weapons and radioactive disasters and how they attempt to cope with such an overwhelming catastrophe. The trauma has been seared into their psyches, and the way the use of nuclear weapons and radioactive disasters are remembered often shapes how the technology is perceived.

They provide clear lessons on how to proceed. Public memory is a powerful force that can influence the trajectory of national policies, and any advancement must be executed with delicacy and respect for those who have suffered the calamitous effects of such fearsome technology.

Small modular reactors

Singapore is exploring the option of nuclear power because our aim is sustainable development. Compared to the carbon emissions from our current fossil fuel-based power, nuclear promises cleaner energy with no carbon emissions.

We have a limited range of options for alternative energy sources. Solar energy is currently being rolled out, but the current technology hasn’t allowed us to rely solely on it. Our geographical location means wind power is not dependable, and our lack of natural resources means we have no feasible access to hydroelectricity.

However, given the history of nuclear disasters above, it is essential that we do not plunge into nuclear power recklessly. An accident would be catastrophic, especially given our tiny territory. A 30 kilometer exclusion zone means most of our densely populated country has to be evacuated, and where would we house the evacuees? This is primarily the reason why, for decades, Singapore has never adopted nuclear power.

Fortunately, nuclear technology has continued to advance and improve. Currently, the development of small modular reactors has made it feasible for Singapore to cautiously proceed on a new path. Compact and scalable, small modular reactors are only a fraction of the size of conventional nuclear reactors, yet they are able to generate up to 300 megawatts per unit. They are safer, equipped with advanced features, such as passive safety systems to reduce risk of accidents, and contain significantly less radioactive fuel. That means, unlike Fukushima, there will be less decay heat to manage if the reactor does have a meltdown. Furthermore, they can be prefabricated for easier transport and installation.

However, the disposal of nuclear waste remains a huge problem in land-scarce Singapore. Worse, a study by Krall, Macfarlane and Ewing discovered that small modular reactors produce between 2 to 30 times more waste per unit of energy than large reactors.[^33] Current options consist of either storage of nuclear waste in a spent fuel pool for between five to fifteen years before sending it to other countries to be extracted, or burying and sealing the nuclear waste in a 2km deep hole.[^34] Perhaps we can explore technology to neutralize radioactive waste, such as nanotechnology. In fact, Belgium’s MYRRHA project is an example of how particle accelerators can transmute waste with long half-lives into shorter-lived material.[^35] Though it’s not ready, perhaps it will be in the future.

Furthermore, Singapore will possibly be able to construct offshore or underground nuclear facilities. This would also mitigate the calamitous effects if any accidents do occur. Theoretically, anyway. In any case, I do think it’s important to consider these three approaches when thinking about nuclear power.

Secure safeguards

Prioritize safety above all.

Combat complacency

Stay vigilant and ensure all measures are in place to prevent accidents.

Reactive response

Have emergency protocols in place to swiftly respond to any issues.

Nuclear disaster in popular imagination

A study of films, games and literature depicting nuclear apocalypses and why it's important. They depict the social anxieties the public face when confronted with nuclear technologies and disasters, and should not be dismissed as merely fiction. Probably Tom Moylan's dystopia books and Timothy Morton's Hyperobjects in academia, and analyses of Japanese films and animation such as Godzilla, Barefoot Gen, Gundam, Akira and many others touch upon it.
