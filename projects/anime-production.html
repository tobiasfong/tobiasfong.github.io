Anime Production Skill

An anime teaser, ad & MV maker for AI coding agents — it turns finished illustrations and a music track into a finished video, entirely on your own machine. It now ships two engines: one that makes still art feel cinematic, and one that makes the art actually move.

What It Is

A skill that teaches the judgment, and a server that does the work

The Anime Production Skill is a self-contained agent skill — a set of instructions plus a bundled rendering engine that an AI coding agent reads and executes on its own. Feed it to your AI harness, hand it your finished illustrations or comic pages and a music track, and it renders an anime teaser, advertisement, or music video: a vertical (9:16) Short, or a landscape (16:9) MV.

It has since grown a companion, anime-production-mcp — an MCP server of 20 tools covering animation, frame editing, drawn effects, retiming, subtitles and assembly. The division is deliberate: the skill teaches judgment, the server does the work. Knowing which shot to animate and which to draw is the part that saves you GPU-hours, so that lives in prose an agent reads; the mechanical steps live in tools it calls. Both are in the same webcomic-toolkit repository, and the server ships 13 GPU-free tests.

It was built and tested with Claude Code, but it isn't tied to any one harness. The instructions are plain markdown (AGENTS.md → SKILL.md) and neither engine has harness-specific dependencies — so it works just as well with OpenAI Codex, Google Antigravity, Gemini CLI, Cursor, or any AGENTS.md-aware agent.

Under the hood: a Remotion (React) pipeline and a Python/Pillow + ffmpeg pipeline, both rendered entirely on your own machine — no cloud service, no watermark, no per-render cost.

Two Engines

Which one you want depends on the shape of the video

The second engine didn't replace the first. They solve different problems, and the skill now opens with a decision table so your agent picks before it starts rather than halfway through:

  Remotion engine Python engine
Shape Vertical 9:16 — Shorts, Reels, TikTok Landscape 16:9 — a full MV
Motion Camera only — Ken Burns, crossfades, particles, a WebGL depth camera The artwork itself moves, plus drawn effects
Editing Timeline you compose in React Cut to a beat grid from the music
Stack Node, Remotion, React Python, Pillow, ffmpeg, ComfyUI — no Node at all
Cost Fast — it's compositing ~65s of GPU per take

One caveat worth stating plainly: the depth camera is Remotion-only, because it's WebGL. The pre-baked parallax MP4s from the Background Generator are a separate thing and drop into either pipeline without Node.

How It Works

One rule decides everything else

The motion in v2 comes from LTX-2.3 22B (distilled, Q4_K_M) running locally in ComfyUI — about 65 seconds per take on a 6GB RTX 3060 Laptop. No subscription, no watermark. But the thing that made it usable rather than a lottery wasn't the model. It was working out what it can and can't be asked for:

LTX relocates what exists. It cannot re-imagine it.

Arm swings, head turns, hair and cloth, drifting snow, fire, camera drift — all fine, because the pixels are already there and only need moving. Blinks fail, because the eyelid was never drawn. Mouth shapes fail, because no teeth or tongue exist in the source. Growing crystals fail, because that's new geometry. So does foreshortening — a punch thrown at the viewer needs knuckles redrawn at an angle that isn't in the illustration. Re-rolling seeds on an impossible ask never works, and that's where the GPU-hours go if you don't know the rule.

Everything else in the pipeline follows from it:

  • Blinks and mouths come from keyframes, not motion. FLUX.1 Kontext generates the closed-eye or open-mouth frame, and only that patch is composited back — Kontext regenerates the whole frame and will quietly restyle your linework otherwise. It's also binary, so half-lids are made by blending the open and closed frames rather than asking for one.
  • Anything that must appear is drawn deterministically. Shooting stars with real occlusion (they pass behind the mansion), growing ice, pond ripples, impact speed lines. Being drawn rather than generated means they can be retimed onto musical beats — and they can't smear a face.
  • Impact is sold, not animated. A punch gets speed lines, a flash and camera shake instead of an animated arm. That's what anime actually does, and it's why no paid image-to-video service was needed for the action beats.

The through-line between the two engines is the same idea at different scales: v1 makes still art feel cinematic by moving the camera; v2 makes the art move where it can, and draws the rest.

Features

What it can do

  • Ken Burns camera motion — slow pans and zooms that give still art a cinematic drift.
  • Crossfades & transitions between illustrations for a smooth, edited feel.
  • Particle effects — shooting stars, embers, drifting petals, and more.
  • Animated video panels — drop in the depth-parallax clips from the background generator as moving panels.
  • Bilingual (EN/JP) credit overlays — title cards and credits in both languages.
  • "Showcase" mode — a Kadokawa-ad-style clean white treatment for cover shots.
  • BGM with fade-out — sync a music track, ending on a clean fade.
  • Zero watermark — the render is yours, clean, at full quality.
  • Locally generated motion — LTX-2.3 in ComfyUI turns a still into a moving shot on a 6GB GPU.
  • Keyframe patching — blinks and mouth shapes via FLUX.1 Kontext, composited back one patch at a time.
  • Drawn effects — shooting stars with real occlusion, growing ice, pond ripples, impact speed lines.
  • Beat-driven editing — cuts land on downbeats, and each shot declares a motion kind (loop / ping-pong / once / hold) that decides its timing.
  • A hand-drawn frame — holds portrait artwork inside a 16:9 canvas, with its leaves and blossoms individually animated so a long credits card never reads as a frozen video.
  • Burned-in bilingual subtitles — JP full size with EN beneath, plus a matching .srt generated from the same cue list so the two can't drift apart.
The Story

Credit goes to taiyousan15

I originally found the Anime Production Skill on the MCP Market, which appears to be a markdown file that listed tools. I downloaded the skill, but realized I had to set up quite a few things, so I decided to rebuild it into something that would make it easier and smoother to use.

My version isn't just a skill — it's now a setup guide that instructs your AI harness to first install the necessary tools and set up the necessary pipeline, together with a complete source engine and bug fixes that I encountered. Feel free to download the skill — the link to my repository is provided below.

Ecosystem

This is part of a broader webcomic/animation ecosystem

This sits alongside my Webcomic Background Generator, Character & Panel Generator, Novel Translation and music generation MCP servers — a wider webcomic/animation ecosystem I've been building piece by piece, with a speech bubble server still to come. They're designed to feed each other. Draw your characters consistently with the panel generator, place them against backgrounds from the background generator, turn those into depth-parallax MP4s that drop straight into this pipeline, and score the result with a track from the music server. The v2 video above is the whole chain in one go: art, song and animation, all produced on the same machine.

For everyone's use

Get the skill and the server

Both are open source (MIT) and built to be portable. Ask your AI harness — Claude Code, Codex, Google Antigravity, Gemini CLI, Cursor, or any AGENTS.md-compatible agent — to clone the webcomic-toolkit repository and follow the setup docs in anime-production-skill. It'll install the engine and render your first video for you. If you want the landscape path with real motion, register anime-production-mcp as well — that one needs ComfyUI and a GPU, and it's happy with 6GB.

Bring your own illustrations and a music track, and let your harness handle the animation. Focus on the art and the story; leave the video editing to the skill.
