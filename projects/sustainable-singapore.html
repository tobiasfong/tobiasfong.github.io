Sustainable Singapore

Tracking the history of climate change in Singapore, and comparing the temperature rise with the rate of urbanization.

A historical overview of temperature rise alongside Singapore's urbanization

Between 1948 and 2025, Singapore grew warmer as the mean surface air temperature rose by an average of 0.25 °C per decade.[^1] That is double the global rate, where temperatures increased by 0.12 °C per decade between 1951 and 2012.[^1]

I'm not a climate scientist, but I am a minor historian, and I'm more comfortable searching archives and studying the past, so that we can look toward the future. As a Singaporean, I'm familiar with how rapidly my country has urbanized since the 1950s — which coincides with what many perceive as the Great Acceleration (though there are others who argue the Great Acceleration began as far back as the Industrial Revolution over a century earlier). The reclamation of Toa Payoh, turning a swamp into a residential area. Expanding our shores to overcome our land scarcity. Kampongs replaced by HDB flats. Grass paved over by concrete. The modernization of Singapore did not come cheap, and much of the cost was nature.

Again, I'm not a climate scientist, and thus, I cannot claim that urbanization caused Singapore to become hotter. However, I think there is something useful in examining the trend that arises when placing the history of urbanization next to a chart of how the temperature has changed over the decades. Sometimes, a historical observation can pick up something that scientific studies might overlook.

Live tracking

Daily temperatures

Below are the temperatures, fetched live from the National Environment Agency's island-wide weather station network at data.gov.sg, showing the gap between the warmest and coolest parts of Singapore in real time.[^4] You can see that the difference can sometimes reach between 2 and 3 °C when comparing urban and vegetated areas.[^29]

For example, in 2023, the difference between Singapore's hottest and coolest stations had a median of 2.2 °C. For over three-fifths of the year, the difference hovered above 2 °C, a measurement I had Claude Code take across 6,893,129 readings (measured per minute) from 17 stations.[^29] When comparing the East Coast Parkway expressway against Pulau Ubin, which remains largely undeveloped to this day, ECP was warmer 78% of the time.

Interestingly enough, the difference is a steady 2.2 to 2.3 °C between 9pm and 7am, only for both temperatures to equalize. In fact, for a short time in the early afternoon, Pulau Ubin appears to be hotter. Apparently, during the day, artificial ground stores the heat before emitting it once night has fallen.

Data

Several Lines of Thought

When I began collecting data (or rather, asked Claude Code to implement the data as a live tracker in this page), I discovered two different records. Since I'm a history guy, I wanted to go as far back as 1900, but only Berkeley Earth's data was available (and goes all the way back to the nineteenth century).[^3] The Berkeley Earth record also ends at 2020, which wouldn't suit my purpose as I wanted a living app that constantly updates every year.

Fortunately, the data from Changi's climate station, which is the official temperature record used by Singapore, is available online on data.gov.sg. However, this same national record only begins in 1982,[^2] which leaves a gap of 82 years. So I'm combining both records to fill in for each other. That said, you'll notice a difference of 0.4 °C between them in the years they overlap. Apparently, Berkeley Earth's record is based on a regional field average, whereas Changi climate station's is much more specific.

The station doesn't remain stationary

Singapore's climate station has constantly moved over the decades, and as you can see from the historical record above, even the nation's land is continuously developing and building. Consequently, the temperature measured will inevitably be entwined with local construction.

Synthesis

Reading the data

From 26.7 °C in 1900, the temperature has climbed to 28.1 °C in 2025, after reaching a height of 28.4 °C the year before. Of note, though, is when Singapore began its rapid urbanization in 1960, when HDB was first formed and began constructing housing for the population. Remarkably, the mean temperature remained consistent during that decade, constantly hovering around 26.7 °C. There are two sharp increases to take note of, such as 26.9 °C in 1966, which if you recall, was when the second five-year program of the Toa Payoh reclamation began, and 27.1 °C in 1969, which was when it neared its completion before people moved in on 1970, which was also a high 26.9 °C.

Aside from 1941, 1958 and 1969, where temperatures hit 27.2, 27.1 and 27.1 °C respectively, Singapore's annual mean never went above 27 °C. That changed from the mid-1960s onward, when temperatures consistently hovered around the 27 °C mark. By 1977, which was well into the expansion of both Changi Airport and Jurong Industrial Estate, Singapore's annual mean hit 27 °C — and on the Changi record, which begins in 1982, it has dropped below that mark only twice since (in 1984, when Godzilla returned, and 1989, when Godzilla fought Biollante).

From the 1990s, temperatures began increasing sharply once more, and though the 1997 high of 28.3 °C is often attributed to the El Niño event that wreaked havoc across the world and continued into 1998, I'm not sure the surge is completely unrelated to Jurong Island's reclamation. Indeed, around 2002, when Jurong Island was on the verge of completion and petrol and fossil fuel companies began occupying it, the temperature shot up to 28.1 °C and slightly dropped to 27.8 °C after its official opening.

In fact, after the 2000s, the temperature has regularly hit 28 °C or above, about 1.1 °C warmer than in 1960. Never mind 1900, over a century ago, in about 60 years, we went from constantly below 27 °C to frequently above 28 °C.

Proposal

Creating a Sustainable Singapore

Of course, Singapore isn't sitting around doing nothing as our little island nation heats up to uncomfortable levels. Urban planners have conducted research, which led them to believe that shading roads with trees can reduce street-level temperatures by 2.6 °C. Furthermore, with buildings under tree shade, air conditioning demand will lessen.[^24]

Lei Xu, Ronita Bardhan, Hao Mei, Srilalitha Gopalakrishnan, Xing Zheng and Thomas Schroepfer's study revealed that street shade does indeed provide effective cooling, with a decrease of 3.1 °C in Universal Thermal Climate Index, 0.8 °C in air temperature, 10.0 °C in mean radiant temperature, and a significant reduction in heat stress severity. Natural shading from trees have been observed to reduce the Universal Thermal Climate Index by up to 10.3 °C, while overhead shading devices lower it by 5.3 °C.[^25] Similarly, Puay Yok Tan, Nyuk Hien Wong, Chun Liang Tan, Steve Kardinal Jusuf, Mei Fen Chang and Zhi Quan Chiam note that vegetation canopy's temperature reduction composes of 29% evaporation cooling and 71% shade, with an average decrease of 1.2 °C.[^28] In other words, tree shade is an effective way of mitigating heat in urban Singapore.

Naika Meili, Xing Zheng, Yuya Takane, Ko Nakajima, Kazuki Yamaguchi, Dengkai Chi, Yue Zhu, Jing Wang, Yeshan Qiu, Athanasios Paschalis, Gabriele Manoli, Paolo Burlando, Puay Yok Tan and Simone Fatichi noted that trees provided a reduction of air conditioner energy consumption of 8% in Singapore, and up to 12% in Tokyo during summer. However, they also observed that planting more trees to get more coverage might not necessarily yield better effects. The optimal cover is 40% tree cover, and anything beyond that will lead to a smaller air conditioner energy consumption reduction.[^26]

Species

What to plant in a tropical city

If shade is doing most of the work, the traits that matter are crown architecture, leaf area index and how fast the tree gets big — plus, in a dense city, whether it can survive compacted soil, road salt-free but pollutant-heavy air, and periodic storms.

TreeWhy it coolsThe catch
Rain tree
Samanea saman		 The classic Singapore canopy: a broad umbrella crown casting dense shade over an entire carriageway. One of the fast-growing “instant trees” the Garden City campaign leaned on to produce results in the shortest possible time — and the species Goh Keng Swee put in the ground at Mount Faber on the first Tree Planting Day.[^19] Needs enormous lateral room; leaflets fold in low light and rain, briefly thinning the shade. Big limbs mean real inspection obligations.
Yellow flame
Peltophorum pterocarpum		 Compared directly against the rain tree in a 2024 Arboricultural Journal study[^27], it transpired more efficiently across cool, warm and hot conditions — the better evaporative cooler of the two. Lighter, less continuous crown than a mature rain tree, so it shades less ground per tree.
Angsana
Pterocarpus indicus		 Dense dome-shaped crown with drooping branches, and fast — the reason it was planted along so many roads in the 1960s and 70s when shade was needed now.[^19] The cautionary tale in Singapore's own greening history: mass single-species planting met vascular wilt disease. An argument for diversity, not for avoiding the species.
Tembusu
Cyrtophyllum fragrans		 Native, long-lived, tough, with a solid dark crown. Handles poor urban soil and holds up structurally. Slow. This is a tree you plant for the next generation's shade, not your own.
Sea apple
Syzygium grande		 Native coastal species, dense evergreen foliage, high leaf area index, wind-firm — well suited to reclaimed and exposed waterfront ground. Moderate spread; better as a street avenue in ranks than as a standalone shade giant.
Dipterocarps
Shorea, Hopea odorata		 The island's original forest canopy. The right answer where the goal is restored habitat and a deep multi-layer canopy, not just a shaded pavement. Slow to establish and demanding of space and soil — a nature-corridor tree, not a roadside one.

The honest caveat on this table

Species-level cooling in Singapore is well studied for a handful of trees — the rain tree and yellow flame comparison is real, measured and citable. For the rest, the reasoning here is from general traits (crown density, leaf area index, growth rate, urban tolerance) and NParks' own planting practice, not from a head-to-head Singapore cooling trial for every row. Treat the first two rows as evidence and the remainder as informed horticultural reasoning.

About The Artwork

No historical photographs were published here

Every illustration on this page was generated locally on my own machine by the Webcomic Background Generator — the MCP server I built for my comic work — running FLUX.1-dev with a manhwa style LoRA.

The research process used archival photographs the way a historian uses them: to find out what a place actually looked like. Toa Payoh before 1962 was attap houses, pig farms and vegetable plots on swampy ground; the East Coast in the 1970s was dredger pipelines discharging sand into shallow sea. Those findings were written into prompts. What is published is a drawn interpretation of a documented scene, not a restyled copy of any photograph — no archival image was used as pixel input, so there is no derivative-work question to answer.

Each plate's seed is recorded, so any image on this page can be regenerated exactly.

Two of the five are derived here rather than read off: the annual mean is the mean of the twelve monthly means, and the annual hottest and coldest readings are the highest and lowest of the twelve monthly extremes. Whether that is a difference of rounding convention or a small difference between this derivation and MSS's homogenized record cannot be settled from outside the agency. The timeline card follows this derived series, so its figures match the chart directly above them; only those two years differ from MSS's table, and only in the last digit. The ten years themselves are not in doubt: both routes select the same set — 1997, 1998, 2002, 2010, 2015, 2016, 2019, 2023, 2024 and 2025 — and in the derived series the tenth place (2002, 28.083 °C) clears the eleventh (2020, 28.042 °C) by more than the rounding at issue. The two do disagree on the order within the ten, which is why the card groups the years by value instead of ranking them. Worth noting: across 1982–2025 the mean daily minimum rises about 0.29 °C per decade against roughly 0.11 °C for the mean daily maximum — the nights warming close to three times as fast as the days. Trends fitted here by least squares on the annual series; they are not MSS figures.

Urban heat island and tree cooling
  1. “Killer Heat”, Sustainable Regional Network, NUS Faculty of Arts and Social Sciences, 3 January 2019 — reporting a study trip with Professor Matthias Roth, NUS Department of Geography, filmed for CNA's Why It Matters 2. “They found that there was a sharp increase of 4°C from Lim Chu Kang to Orchard Road, despite these areas being only 30 minutes apart.” Roth attributes the difference to two mechanisms: background global warming and, more importantly, the urban heat island — Lim Chu Kang being open secondary rainforest and farmland, Orchard Road being tall buildings, little vegetation and asphalt. A note on verification

Not all of these were checked to the same depth, and it would be dishonest to present them as though they were. References 1–5 were downloaded and inspected directly — the temperature and land-area series on this page are computed from the raw files, not copied from anyone's chart. Every other reference was read on the cited page, and references 23, 25, 26 and 28 were read in full text rather than from their abstracts — so the 3.1 °C thermal-comfort figure and the 40%-canopy threshold now come from the papers themselves rather than from a summary of them. Reference 12 is a blog rather than an official source, and is attributed as such in the text. Reference 27 is the one remaining weak link: it is paywalled and only its abstract has been read.

The live figures on this page come from the data.gov.sg public APIs, called directly from your browser. There is no server in between — no backend, no API key, nothing stored.
