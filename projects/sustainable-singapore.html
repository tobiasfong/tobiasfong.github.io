Environmental History

Sustainable Singapore

Singapore has warmed at roughly twice the global rate. The interesting historical question is not whether it warmed — it's how much of that heat is the planet's, and how much the island built for itself.

The Question

Two curves, one island

Between 1948 and 2025, the Meteorological Service Singapore records the island's mean surface air temperature rising by an average of 0.25 °C per decade.[^1] Over a comparable stretch the global figure was about 0.12 °C per decade.[^1] Singapore warmed about twice as fast as the world.

A climate scientist reaches for attribution models. A historian reaches for the other archive — the one made of reclamation contracts, five-year building programmes and tree-planting campaigns. Because over exactly the same period, Singapore did something few countries have done at this speed: it rebuilt its own surface. Swamp became new town. Sea became land. Kampong became concrete. Every one of those decisions changed how the ground absorbs, stores and releases heat.

So this page puts the two archives side by side, on one shared vertical axis: what the island built, and what the thermometers read. It does not claim the first caused all of the second — the honest position, and the one the national weather service itself takes, is more careful than that. But the two tracks are worth reading together.

Method

Why there are two temperature lines

The official Singapore series everyone quotes — the one behind "0.25 °C per decade since 1948" — is a homogenised national record. The clean, downloadable, machine-readable version of it on data.gov.sg only begins in 1982[^2], when the designated climate station moved to Changi. That is a real limit, and pretending otherwise would be the easiest way to mislead on a page like this.

So the long line is a second, independent reconstruction: Berkeley Earth's regional average for Singapore, which extends back to the nineteenth century.[^3] It runs about 0.4 °C below the Changi station's absolute reading across their overlap. That offset is a baseline difference — a regional field average versus one station's thermometer — not a disagreement about direction. Both are drawn, at their own levels, rather than spliced into one tidy line that would hide the seam.

The station itself moved as the city grew

This is the awkward, genuinely interesting problem at the heart of any urban temperature record. Singapore's climate station has not sat still: the reference site shifted over the decades, and the land around every candidate site was being built on the whole time. A thermometer that stays put while a city rises around it measures the city. A thermometer that moves to escape the city measures something else. Neither is "wrong", but it means the raw record can never cleanly separate global warming from local construction — which is precisely why the national weather service hedges its own attribution.

The Remedy

Putting the canopy back

There is a neat historical symmetry here. The same decades that stripped the island's surface also produced the instinct to re-cover it. On 16 June 1963, barely three years into the housing programme that was clearing kampongs at speed, Lee Kuan Yew planted a mempat tree at Farrer Circus and launched the first tree-planting campaign.[^13] The "Garden City" campaign followed in 1967; the first Tree Planting Day in November 1971.[^14]

The species he pushed hardest — the angsana and the rain tree — were not chosen for flowers. They were chosen for crown: broad, dense, fast-growing canopies that could be thrown over a hot new road quickly. The stated aim was a cool canopy over the city. It was, in retrospect, an urban-heat-island intervention launched decades before the term was in common local use.

The current instalment is OneMillionTrees, run by NParks under the Singapore Green Plan 2030: a million additional trees planted between 2020 and 2030, currently expected to hit target by the end of 2027.[^15]

The Evidence

How much do trees actually cool?

Less than the headline number suggests, and more than the air temperature alone shows. Both halves of that sentence matter.

The figure most often quoted to Singapore's planners is a reduction of up to 2.6 °C in street-level temperature from shading roads with trees, via the Strategies for Cooling Singapore report. Worth knowing before you repeat it: that range originates in a Dresden study, not in Singapore measurements.[^18]

When Lei Xu and Ronita Bardhan actually measured 20 paired shaded/unshaded sites across Singapore (published in Science of the Total Environment, December 2024), the effect on air temperature was modest — close to a single degree. But the effect on thermal comfort, measured on the Universal Thermal Climate Index, averaged 3.1 °C. Under high coverage — roughly 80% canopy and built overhangs — the modelled cooling potential reached 3–5 °C.[^19]

That gap between "air temperature" and "how hot it feels" is the whole point. A tree does not primarily cool the air; it intercepts the radiation that would have cooked you and the pavement. Roughly 29% of the in-canopy temperature reduction comes from evaporative cooling — shade does the rest.[^22] For a pedestrian in the tropics, the radiant load is what matters, and that is exactly what a crown blocks.

There is also a limit worth planning around. Naika Meili's 2025 modelling in the Journal of Advances in Modeling Earth Systems[^20] found shade on walls and windows dominated air-conditioning savings — 6–9% of cooling energy in hot humid cities — but that the benefit peaks at around 40% tree cover in humid climates, beyond which the moisture the trees release starts offsetting the gain. In a place as humid as Singapore, more canopy is not linearly better.

Species

What to plant in a tropical city

If shade is doing most of the work, the traits that matter are crown architecture, leaf area index and how fast the tree gets big — plus, in a dense city, whether it can survive compacted soil, road salt-free but pollutant-heavy air, and periodic storms.

TreeWhy it coolsThe catch
Rain tree
Samanea saman		 The classic Singapore canopy: a broad umbrella crown that can span 30 m, casting dense shade over an entire carriageway. LKY's favourite for exactly this reason.[^13] Needs enormous lateral room; leaflets fold in low light and rain, briefly thinning the shade. Big limbs mean real inspection obligations.
Yellow flame
Peltophorum pterocarpum		 Compared directly against the rain tree in a 2024 Arboricultural Journal study[^21], it transpired more efficiently across cool, warm and hot conditions — the better evaporative cooler of the two. Lighter, less continuous crown than a mature rain tree, so it shades less ground per tree.
Angsana
Pterocarpus indicus		 Dense dome-shaped crown with drooping branches, and fast — the reason it was planted along so many roads in the 1960s and 70s when shade was needed now.[^13] The cautionary tale in Singapore's own greening history: mass single-species planting met vascular wilt disease. An argument for diversity, not for avoiding the species.
Tembusu
Cyrtophyllum fragrans		 Native, long-lived, tough, with a solid dark crown. Handles poor urban soil and holds up structurally. Slow. This is a tree you plant for the next generation's shade, not your own.
Sea apple
Syzygium grande		 Native coastal species, dense evergreen foliage, high leaf area index, wind-firm — well suited to reclaimed and exposed waterfront ground. Moderate spread; better as a street avenue in ranks than as a standalone shade giant.
Dipterocarps
Shorea, Hopea odorata		 The island's original forest canopy. The right answer where the goal is restored habitat and a deep multi-layer canopy, not just a shaded pavement. Slow to establish and demanding of space and soil — a nature-corridor tree, not a roadside one.

The honest caveat on this table

Species-level cooling in Singapore is well studied for a handful of trees — the rain tree and yellow flame comparison is real, measured and citable. For the rest, the reasoning here is from general traits (crown density, leaf area index, growth rate, urban tolerance) and NParks' own planting practice, not from a head-to-head Singapore cooling trial for every row. Treat the first two rows as evidence and the remainder as informed horticultural reasoning.

About The Artwork

No historical photographs were published here

Every illustration on this page was generated locally on my own machine by the Webcomic Background Generator — the MCP server I built for my comic work — running FLUX.1-dev with a manhwa style LoRA.

The research process used archival photographs the way a historian uses them: to find out what a place actually looked like. Toa Payoh before 1962 was attap houses, pig farms and vegetable plots on swampy ground; the East Coast in the 1970s was dredger pipelines discharging sand into shallow sea. Those findings were written into prompts. What is published is a drawn interpretation of a documented scene, not a restyled copy of any photograph — no archival image was used as pixel input, so there is no derivative-work question to answer.

Each plate's seed is recorded, so any image on this page can be regenerated exactly.
